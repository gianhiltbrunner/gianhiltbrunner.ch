Tantanmen is not really a Japanese invention, and it isn't really Sichuanese either any more. Chen Kenmin brought dan dan mian with him when he opened Shisen Hanten in Tokyo in 1957 — dry noodles, chili oil, preserved greens, a spoonful of pork. Japanese diners wanted noodles in soup, so broth was added, sesame paste moved to the centre of the seasoning, and within a few decades the dish had become its own thing. The best version I've had is at [Nakiryu](https://tabelog.com/en/tokyo/A1323/A132302/13141302/) in Otsuka, where the chili oil and sesame sit in a thick layer on top of a light, faintly sour broth, and the minced pork does most of the heavy lifting.

That last part is the problem with a vegetarian tantanmen, and it's the same problem Mike Satinover names in *The Book of Ramen*: plant-based soups lack viscosity and umami. You can fix umami with kombu and shiitake. Viscosity is harder. This version solves it the way most Japanese shops already do — with soy milk — and replaces the pork soboro with a mushroom and walnut version that browns hard enough to read as meaty.

Compared to the version of this recipe I published first, three things changed and they all matter: the soup is now part chintan and part soy milk instead of straight stock, there is a soboro (a tantanmen without one is really just a sesame ramen), and the tare carries doubanjiang, black vinegar and raw garlic rather than being sesame and soy sauce alone. The chili oil is also bloomed with hot oil instead of steeped off the heat, which is the difference between an oil that is faintly spicy and one that is red.

## Sources

- *The Book of Ramen*; Michael T. Satinover and Scott J. Satinover
- The Shisen Hanten lineage — Chen Kenmin, Chen Kenichi and Chen Kentaro — for the shape of the Japanese dish
- Sichuan dan dan mian, for the black vinegar, the preserved mustard greens and the huajiao

## Tantanmen Bowl

Makes 1 bowl

- 200 milliliters vegetable chintan, hot
- 150 milliliters (about 2/3 cup) unsweetened soy milk, warmed to about 70°C
- 70 grams tantanmen tare
- 20 milliliters (4 teaspoons) la-yu, oil and sediment
- 130 grams ramen noodles, cooked ([Tokyo-style noodles](/posts/tokyo-style-noodle/) at 38% hydration work well)
- 60 grams mushroom and walnut soboro, hot
- 1 head pak choi, blanched
- Spice blend, to taste (start with 1 teaspoon)
- Toppings: sliced scallions, toasted crushed peanuts, freshly ground Sichuan pepper, 1 half-cooked egg (optional)

1. Preheat the serving bowl. Ramen shops fill the bowl with boiling noodle water; at home, put the bowls in the oven at its lowest setting while you work. A cold bowl steals 20-30°C from the soup on contact.
2. Add the tare, la-yu, and spice blend to the bottom of the bowl. Whisk briefly — the tare is thick and needs help.
3. Pour in the hot chintan and whisk until the tare is fully dispersed and the soup looks uniform.
4. Stir in the warm soy milk. Taste now and adjust with a little more tare or a splash of chintan.
5. Cook the noodles in rapidly boiling water, strain hard, and lay them into the bowl.
6. Top with the soboro at 6 o'clock, the pak choi beside it, the egg if using, then scallions and crushed peanuts.
7. Grind Sichuan pepper over the top and eat immediately.

**On Blending Versus Layering**: The original version of this recipe put everything in a blender and ran it at high speed, which produces a completely homogeneous, café-au-lait soup — creamy, unified, and a little flat. Nakiryu does the opposite: the chili oil and sesame float in a distinct layer over a lighter broth, so the first sip is fierce and sesame-heavy and the bowl mellows as you eat. Whisking in the bowl, as above, sits between the two and is what I'd recommend. If you want the blended version, blend the chintan and tare only, then stir the soy milk in by hand — blending soy milk aerates it into foam.

**On Soy Milk**: Use unsweetened, and don't boil it. Soy protein curdles when acid and heat arrive together, and this tare carries both vinegar and fermented bean paste. Warm the soy milk to about 70°C in a separate pan and add it after the chintan has already diluted the tare. If it does split, the bowl still tastes fine — it just looks grainy.

**On Salinity**: Satinover's target is around 1.5% salt across the finished bowl. 70 grams of this tare puts roughly 4.7 grams of salt into 350 milliliters, which lands near 1.35%, with the soboro contributing the rest. That is deliberate — the sesame and soy milk blunt salt perception, so a bowl that measures correctly can still taste underseasoned. Taste at step 4 rather than trusting the number.

## Vegetable Chintan

Makes about 7 bowls

- 3 liters water
- 1 onion, split and peeled
- 1 carrot, peeled and cut into large chunks
- 10 cloves garlic
- 1 5-centimeter piece of ginger
- 10 grams dried shiitake
- 1 bunch of green onions
- 20 grams kombu

Vegetarian soups run into two problems: no viscosity and no umami. This chintan solves the second by pairing kombu (glutamic acid) with dried shiitake (guanylic acid), which together produce far more perceived umami than either alone. The first problem gets solved in the bowl, by the soy milk and the sesame paste.

1. Add the water, onion, carrot, garlic, ginger, and shiitake to a pot.
2. Bring to a boil over high heat, then reduce to a simmer.
3. Cook at a gentle simmer (around 95°C) for one hour.
4. Add the green onion and kombu, turn off the heat, and let steep for 15 minutes.
5. Strain the soup and reserve until needed.

Use 200 milliliters per bowl. The stock keeps for 3-4 days in the fridge and freezes well.

## Tantanmen Tare

Makes about 6 bowls (roughly 415 grams)

- 150 grams nerigoma (Japanese sesame paste) or tahini
- 165 grams soy sauce
- 30 grams (2 tablespoons) Chinkiang black vinegar
- 25 grams doubanjiang, finely chopped
- 15 grams sugar
- 12 grams (1 tablespoon) toasted sesame oil
- 10 grams MSG
- 8 grams garlic (about 2 cloves), grated

The book's tantanmen tare is deliberately minimal — soy sauce, rice vinegar, ground sesame — and it works, but it leaves the dish's Sichuan half on the table. Satinover himself suggests folding in other common ingredients such as Sichuan pickles. This version keeps his ratio of sesame to soy sauce roughly intact and adds the three things a Chengdu bowl would never be without: fermented broad bean paste, black vinegar, and raw garlic.

1. Put the sesame paste in a bowl and add the soy sauce in three additions, whisking each one in completely before adding the next. Added all at once, the paste seizes and refuses to come together.
2. Whisk in the black vinegar, doubanjiang, sugar, sesame oil, MSG, and garlic.
3. Rest in the fridge for at least a few hours before use, and preferably overnight — the raw garlic needs time to lose its edge.

Use 70 grams per bowl. Keeps for 3 weeks refrigerated. The paste separates on standing; whisk before each use.

**On Sesame Paste**: Nerigoma is made from Japanese-style roasted sesame and is darker, sweeter and much more aromatic than tahini, which is usually ground from lightly toasted or raw seed. Tahini works, and the book allows for it, but the bowl comes out noticeably paler in flavour. If you only have tahini, toast 20 grams of white sesame seeds in a dry pan and grind them into the tare to make up the difference. Chinese sesame paste (zhima jiang) is the closest substitute of all.

**On Black Vinegar**: Chinkiang vinegar is malty and slightly sweet where rice vinegar is just sharp, and it is what gives a good tantanmen the faint sourness underneath the sesame — the same note that runs through Nakiryu's bowl. It's doing structural work here: without acid, a soup this rich in sesame fat and soy milk goes cloying about half a bowl in.

## Mushroom and Walnut Soboro

Makes about 6 servings

- 30 milliliters (2 tablespoons) neutral vegetable oil
- 350 grams fresh shiitake or chestnut mushrooms, finely chopped
- 20 grams dried shiitake, rehydrated and minced (reserve the soaking liquid)
- 80 grams walnuts, toasted and coarsely chopped
- Half of one white onion, finely diced
- 2 cloves garlic, minced
- 8 grams ginger, minced
- 20 grams doubanjiang
- 35 grams (about 2 tablespoons) tianmianjiang (sweet bean paste), or red miso
- 30 milliliters (2 tablespoons) soy sauce
- 15 milliliters (1 tablespoon) mirin
- 15 milliliters (1 tablespoon) Shaoxing wine or sake
- 40 grams sui mi ya cai or zha cai, rinsed and chopped (optional but worth finding)
- 5 milliliters (1 teaspoon) toasted sesame oil
- White pepper and salt, to taste

This follows the structure of the book's pork soboro — aromatics, protein, then soy sauce, sweet bean paste, mirin and sake cooked down until glossy — with mushrooms and walnuts standing in for the pork. The walnuts matter more than they look like they should: mushrooms alone go soft and uniform, while chopped walnuts keep a fatty, slightly crumbly bite that reads as ground meat.

1. Heat a wide pan over high heat with no oil. Add the fresh mushrooms and dry-fry, stirring, until they have given up their water and it has fully evaporated, 8-12 minutes. Keep going until they squeak and start to brown in patches.
2. Add the oil, then the onion, and cook over medium heat until the onion is translucent and the mushrooms are properly browned, 5 minutes more.
3. Add the garlic, ginger, and rehydrated dried shiitake. Cook until fragrant, about 1 minute.
4. Push everything to one side, add the doubanjiang to the bare part of the pan, and fry it in the oil for 30-60 seconds until the oil turns red and smells sweet, then stir it through.
5. Add the walnuts, tianmianjiang, soy sauce, mirin, and Shaoxing wine along with 50 milliliters of the shiitake soaking liquid. Cook, stirring, until the liquid has reduced and the mixture is glossy rather than wet, 5-8 minutes.
6. Stir in the ya cai and sesame oil. Season with white pepper and salt.

Use about 60 grams per bowl. Keeps 5 days in the fridge and freezes well in portions.

**On Dry-Frying the Mushrooms**: Fresh mushrooms are roughly 90% water, and that water has to leave before anything can brown. Adding oil first just steams them in fat and produces a grey, slippery mince. Dry-frying drives the water off first, so the Maillard reaction can actually happen when the oil goes in — this single step is most of the difference between a mushroom mince that tastes like a substitute and one that tastes like a topping.

**On Frying the Doubanjiang**: Pixian doubanjiang is fried in oil in essentially every Sichuan dish that uses it, and the reason is visible: the pigments and aroma compounds are fat-soluble, so they only fully release into the oil. Stirred in raw at the end, it tastes flat and salty. Given 30 seconds in hot oil, it stains everything red and turns sweet.

**On Ya Cai**: Sui mi ya cai — finely chopped preserved mustard greens from Yibin — is the ingredient that makes dan dan noodles taste like dan dan noodles. It's salty, faintly sweet, and deeply funky. Sichuan zha cai (preserved mustard stem) is a decent stand-in and easier to find. Rinse either one before chopping, or the soboro ends up too salty.

## La-Yu

Makes about 250 milliliters

**Aromatic oil:**

- 250 milliliters (1 cup) neutral vegetable oil
- 8 cloves garlic
- 1 5-centimeter piece of ginger, sliced into 0.5-centimeter coins
- Half of a small onion
- 1 star anise
- 1 small piece cassia bark or cinnamon
- 1 bay leaf

**Chili mixture:**

- 25 grams (about 3 tablespoons) coarsely ground togarashi
- 4 dried Chinese chilis, ground
- 10 grams toasted white sesame seeds
- 5 grams (1 tablespoon) Sichuan peppercorns, toasted and coarsely ground
- 15 milliliters (1 tablespoon) toasted sesame oil

The book's chili oil steeps the spices in oil that has been taken off the heat. That produces a fragrant oil, but a pale one. Pouring hot oil over the ground chili instead extracts the capsaicin and the red pigments properly, and it is what makes a chili oil look like the stuff on top of a shop bowl. The sediment is not a byproduct here — it's half the point, so this one doesn't get strained at the end.

1. In a small saucepan, combine the oil, garlic, ginger, onion, star anise, cassia, and bay leaf.
2. Heat over medium to medium-low, stirring occasionally, until the aromatics take on a light golden hue and smell fragrant, around 15 minutes.
3. Strain out the solids and return the oil to the pan. Discard the aromatics.
4. Combine the togarashi, ground Chinese chilis, and sesame seeds in a heatproof bowl — metal or ceramic, not glass.
5. Heat the oil to 150°C. If you don't have a thermometer, a pinch of the chili mixture dropped in should sizzle vigorously and go fragrant within a couple of seconds without instantly blackening.
6. Pour half the oil over the chili mixture. It will foam up hard — stir it down. Wait one minute, then pour in the rest.
7. When the oil has cooled below 120°C, stir in the ground Sichuan peppercorns and the sesame oil.
8. Let the oil sit at room temperature for at least 12 hours, then transfer to a jar, sediment included. Keeps refrigerated for 6 months.

Use 20 milliliters per bowl, stirred up so you get sediment as well as oil.

**On Pouring Temperature**: Above about 175°C ground chili scorches in seconds and the oil turns bitter and brown. Below about 120°C it never blooms and you get a mild orange oil. 150°C, poured in two additions so the temperature drops between them, extracts colour and heat without burning. The two-stage pour also keeps the sesame seeds from going black.

**On Sichuan Pepper**: The tingling comes from hydroxy-alpha-sanshool, which degrades with sustained heat — which is why the peppercorns go in after the oil has cooled rather than getting fried with everything else. It's also why grinding a little fresh over the finished bowl is worth doing even though there is already huajiao in the oil. Toast the whole peppercorns in a dry pan for a minute before grinding, and sift out the gritty black seeds if you're being careful; they contribute nothing but sand.

## Spice Blend

- 8 dried Chinese chilis, whole
- 6 grams (2 tablespoons) togarashi
- 3 grams (1 tablespoon) whole Sichuan peppercorns
- 2 grams (2 teaspoons) white peppercorns, optional

Straight from the book, and the thing that lets you dial heat per bowl without rebuilding the tare. Grind each ingredient separately in a spice mill, then combine. Add a teaspoon to the bottom of the bowl with the tare, and more on top of the finished soup if you want it hotter.

## Pak Choi

- 1 small head pak choi per bowl
- Salt

1. Trim the base and separate the leaves, halving any large ones lengthwise.
2. Blanch in the noodle water (before the noodles go in) for 60-90 seconds, until the stems are tender but still snappy.
3. Lift out, shake dry thoroughly, and season with a pinch of salt.

Blanching in the noodle water is not laziness — the alkaline water keeps the greens vividly green. Shake them properly dry; water clinging to the leaves dilutes the bowl.

## Half-Cooked Eggs

Optional, and the one non-vegan element in the bowl. Leave it out and everything above is fully plant-based.

- Refrigerated eggs

These ajitsuke tamago (seasoned eggs) feature a jammy yolk with a firm white, marinated in a savory-sweet soy mixture. The ice bath stops the cooking process instantly, ensuring the perfect texture every time.

1. Bring a large pot of water to a full boil.
2. In a large bowl or container, fill with ice and cold water to make an ice bath. Reserve.
3. Remove eggs from the refrigerator.
4. Working in batches of 4-6 eggs at a time to avoid the water temperature dropping too quickly, lower your eggs into the boiling water.
5. Boil the eggs, stirring occasionally during the first minute or so of cooking, for 6-7 minutes, depending on how firm you'd like the yolk. I prefer 7 minutes for a slightly custardy yolk. For a completely liquid yolk, cook for 6 to 6.5 minutes.
6. After the time is up, quickly remove the eggs from the water and place them in the ice bath from step 2. Hold for 15 minutes, or until thoroughly chilled.

### Marinade

- One part soy sauce
- One part mirin
- One part water

1. Place peeled eggs in an airtight container, such as a hard plastic container or a sealable bag.
2. Pour the above ingredients into the container to cover (you may not need to use all the brine; reduce the amount as necessary).
3. Soak, jostling contents every hour or so, for 4-6 hours.
4. Remove from brine and reserve in the fridge until needed.
