Tantanmen is not really a Japanese invention, and it isn't really Sichuanese any more either. Chen Kenmin brought dan dan mian with him when he opened Shisen Hanten in Tokyo in 1957 — dry noodles, chili oil, preserved greens, a spoonful of pork. Japanese diners wanted noodles in soup, so broth was added, sesame paste moved to the centre of the seasoning, and within a few decades the dish had become its own thing. The best version I've had is at [Nakiryu](https://tabelog.com/en/tokyo/A1323/A132302/13141302/) in Otsuka, where the chili oil and sesame sit in a thick layer over a light, faintly sour broth, and the minced pork does most of the heavy lifting.

That last part is the problem with a vegetarian tantanmen. Plant-based soups have no viscosity and not much umami. Umami you can fix with kombu and dried shiitake. Viscosity is harder, and this version solves it the way most Japanese shops already do — with soy milk. The pork gets replaced with a spicy mince that takes about ten minutes.

Compared to the version I first published, three things changed and they all matter: the soup is now part chintan and part soy milk instead of straight stock, there is a mince on top (a tantanmen without one is really just a sesame ramen), and the tare carries doubanjiang, black vinegar and raw garlic rather than being sesame and soy sauce alone.

## Tantanmen Bowl

Makes 1 bowl

- 200 milliliters vegetable chintan, hot
- 150 milliliters (about 2/3 cup) unsweetened soy milk, warmed to about 70°C
- 70 grams tantanmen tare
- 15 milliliters (1 tablespoon) chili oil
- 130 grams ramen noodles, cooked ([Tokyo-style noodles](/posts/tokyo-style-noodle/) at 38% hydration work well)
- 60 grams spicy mince, hot
- 1 head pak choi, blanched
- Toppings: sliced scallions, toasted crushed peanuts, freshly ground Sichuan pepper and togarashi to taste, 1 half-cooked egg (optional)

1. Preheat the serving bowl. Ramen shops fill the bowl with boiling noodle water; at home, put the bowls in the oven at its lowest setting while you work. A cold bowl steals 20-30°C from the soup on contact.
2. Add the tare and chili oil to the bottom of the bowl. Whisk briefly — the tare is thick and needs help.
3. Pour in the hot chintan and whisk until the tare is fully dispersed and the soup looks uniform.
4. Stir in the warm soy milk. Taste now and adjust with a little more tare or a splash of chintan.
5. Cook the noodles in rapidly boiling water, strain hard, and lay them into the bowl.
6. Top with the mince at 6 o'clock, the pak choi beside it, the egg if using, then scallions and crushed peanuts.
7. Grind Sichuan pepper over the top, add togarashi if you want more heat, and eat immediately.

**On Whisking Rather Than Blending**: The original version of this recipe put everything in a blender and ran it at high speed, which produces a completely homogeneous, café-au-lait soup — creamy, unified, and a little flat. Nakiryu does the opposite: the chili oil and sesame float in a distinct layer over a lighter broth, so the first sip is fierce and sesame-heavy and the bowl mellows as you eat. Whisking in the bowl sits between the two and is what I'd recommend. If you do want the blended version, blend the chintan and tare only and stir the soy milk in by hand — blending soy milk aerates it into foam.

**On Soy Milk**: Use unsweetened, and don't boil it. Soy protein curdles when acid and heat arrive together, and this tare carries both vinegar and fermented bean paste. Warm the soy milk in a separate pan to about 70°C and add it after the chintan has already diluted the tare. If it does split, the bowl still tastes fine — it just looks grainy.

**On Salinity**: A finished bowl wants to land somewhere around 1.5% salt. 70 grams of this tare puts roughly 4.7 grams into 350 milliliters, which is about 1.35%, with the mince contributing the rest. That is deliberate — sesame and soy milk blunt salt perception, so a bowl that measures correctly can still taste underseasoned. Taste at step 4 rather than trusting the number.

## Vegetable Chintan

Makes about 7 bowls

- 3 liters water
- 1 onion, split and peeled
- 1 carrot, peeled and cut into large chunks
- 10 cloves garlic
- 1 5-centimeter piece of ginger
- 10 grams dried shiitake
- 1 bunch of green onions
- 20 grams kombu

Vegetarian soups run into two problems: no viscosity and no umami. This chintan solves the second by pairing kombu (glutamic acid) with dried shiitake (guanylic acid), which together produce far more perceived umami than either does alone. The first problem gets solved in the bowl, by the soy milk and the sesame paste.

1. Add the water, onion, carrot, garlic, ginger, and shiitake to a pot.
2. Bring to a boil over high heat, then reduce to a simmer.
3. Cook at a gentle simmer (around 95°C) for one hour.
4. Add the green onion and kombu, turn off the heat, and let steep for 15 minutes.
5. Strain the soup and reserve until needed.

Use 200 milliliters per bowl. The stock keeps for 3-4 days in the fridge and freezes well.

## Tantanmen Tare

Makes about 6 bowls (roughly 415 grams)

- 150 grams nerigoma (Japanese sesame paste) or tahini
- 165 grams soy sauce
- 30 grams (2 tablespoons) Chinkiang black vinegar
- 25 grams doubanjiang, finely chopped
- 15 grams sugar
- 12 grams (1 tablespoon) toasted sesame oil
- 10 grams MSG
- 8 grams garlic (about 2 cloves), grated

A minimal tantanmen tare is just sesame paste, soy sauce and vinegar, and that does work. But it leaves the dish's Sichuan half on the table, so this one adds the three things a Chengdu bowl would never be without: fermented broad bean paste, black vinegar, and raw garlic.

1. Put the sesame paste in a bowl and add the soy sauce in three additions, whisking each one in completely before adding the next. Added all at once, the paste seizes and refuses to come together.
2. Whisk in the black vinegar, doubanjiang, sugar, sesame oil, MSG, and garlic.
3. Rest in the fridge for at least a few hours before use, and preferably overnight — the raw garlic needs time to lose its edge.

Use 70 grams per bowl. Keeps for 3 weeks refrigerated. The paste separates on standing, so whisk before each use.

**On Sesame Paste**: Nerigoma is made from Japanese-style roasted sesame and is darker, sweeter and much more aromatic than tahini, which is usually ground from lightly toasted or raw seed. Tahini works, but the bowl comes out noticeably paler in flavour. If tahini is all you have, toast 20 grams of white sesame seeds in a dry pan and grind them into the tare to make up the difference. Chinese sesame paste (zhima jiang) is the closest substitute of all.

**On Black Vinegar**: Chinkiang vinegar is malty and slightly sweet where rice vinegar is just sharp, and it is what gives a good tantanmen the faint sourness underneath the sesame — the same note that runs through Nakiryu's bowl. It's doing structural work here: without acid, a soup this rich in sesame fat and soy milk goes cloying about half a bowl in.

## Spicy Mince

Makes about 6 servings

- 15 milliliters (1 tablespoon) neutral vegetable oil
- 300 grams plant-based mince
- 2 cloves garlic, minced
- 8 grams ginger, minced
- 20 grams doubanjiang
- 30 grams (2 tablespoons) tianmianjiang (sweet bean paste), or red miso
- 20 milliliters (4 teaspoons) soy sauce
- 15 milliliters (1 tablespoon) mirin
- 40 grams sui mi ya cai or zha cai, rinsed and chopped (optional, and the most authentic thing you can add)
- White pepper, to taste

Store-bought mince is the shortcut that makes this a weeknight bowl rather than a project. The seasoning does the work: fermented bean pastes, soy sauce and mirin cooked down until the mixture is glossy rather than wet.

1. Heat the oil in a wide pan over medium-high heat. Add the mince and cook, breaking up clumps, until browned in patches, 5-7 minutes. Let it sit still for a minute at a time — constant stirring steams it instead of browning it.
2. Add the garlic and ginger and cook until fragrant, about 1 minute.
3. Push everything to one side, add the doubanjiang to the bare part of the pan, and fry it for 30-60 seconds until the oil turns red and smells sweet, then stir it through.
4. Add the tianmianjiang, soy sauce, and mirin. Cook, stirring, until the liquid has reduced and the mince is glossy, 2-3 minutes.
5. Stir in the ya cai if using, and season with white pepper.

Use about 60 grams per bowl. Keeps 5 days in the fridge and freezes well in portions.

**On Frying the Doubanjiang**: Pixian doubanjiang gets fried in oil in essentially every Sichuan dish that uses it, and the reason is visible: its pigments and aroma compounds are fat-soluble, so they only fully release into the oil. Stirred in raw at the end it tastes flat and salty. Given 30 seconds in hot oil it stains everything red and turns sweet.

**On Ya Cai**: Sui mi ya cai — finely chopped preserved mustard greens from Yibin — is the ingredient that makes dan dan noodles taste like dan dan noodles: salty, faintly sweet, deeply funky. Sichuan zha cai (preserved mustard stem) is a decent stand-in and easier to find. Rinse either one before chopping or the mince ends up too salty.

## Chili Oil

Makes about 6 bowls

This is the same aromatic oil as in my [yuzu shio ramen](/posts/yuzu-shio-ramen/) — the gentle infusion suits tantanmen just as well, and it's worth making a double batch so one jar covers both bowls.

- 100 milliliters vegetable oil (neutral flavor, such as grapeseed or canola)
- 1 5-centimeter piece of fresh ginger, sliced into thin coins
- 4 cloves garlic, smashed
- 2-3 dried red chilis, whole or broken
- 2-3 green onions (scallions), white and light green parts only, cut into 3-centimeter pieces

1. In a small saucepan, combine the oil, ginger, garlic, and chilis.
2. Place over medium-low heat and warm gently. You should see small bubbles forming around the aromatics, but not aggressive frying.
3. Cook for 15-20 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the ginger and garlic are golden and fragrant. Watch carefully to prevent burning.
4. Remove from heat and immediately add the green onions. The residual heat will cook them gently without burning.
5. Let the aromatics steep in the oil for 10 minutes.
6. Strain through a fine-mesh strainer into a clean container, discarding the solids.

Use 15 milliliters (1 tablespoon) per bowl. Store at room temperature for up to 1 week, or refrigerate for up to 1 month.

**Making It Redder**: This oil is fragrant rather than fiery, which suits a bowl that already has doubanjiang in the tare. If you want the red slick you see on a shop bowl, stir a tablespoon of coarsely ground togarashi into the oil while it is still warm — around 120°C, hot enough to bloom the colour but not hot enough to scorch it — and leave the sediment in the jar rather than straining it out.

## Pak Choi

- 1 small head pak choi per bowl
- Salt

1. Trim the base and separate the leaves, halving any large ones lengthwise.
2. Blanch in the noodle water, before the noodles go in, for 60-90 seconds, until the stems are tender but still snappy.
3. Lift out, shake dry thoroughly, and season with a pinch of salt.

Blanching in the noodle water is not laziness — the alkaline water keeps the greens vividly green. Shake them properly dry; water clinging to the leaves dilutes the bowl.

## Half-Cooked Eggs

Optional, and the one non-vegan element in the bowl. Leave it out and everything above is fully plant-based.

- Refrigerated eggs

These ajitsuke tamago (seasoned eggs) feature a jammy yolk with a firm white, marinated in a savory-sweet soy mixture. The ice bath stops the cooking process instantly, ensuring the perfect texture every time.

1. Bring a large pot of water to a full boil.
2. In a large bowl or container, fill with ice and cold water to make an ice bath. Reserve.
3. Remove eggs from the refrigerator.
4. Working in batches of 4-6 eggs at a time to avoid the water temperature dropping too quickly, lower your eggs into the boiling water.
5. Boil the eggs, stirring occasionally during the first minute or so of cooking, for 6-7 minutes, depending on how firm you'd like the yolk. I prefer 7 minutes for a slightly custardy yolk. For a completely liquid yolk, cook for 6 to 6.5 minutes.
6. After the time is up, quickly remove the eggs from the water and place them in the ice bath from step 2. Hold for 15 minutes, or until thoroughly chilled.

### Marinade

- One part soy sauce
- One part mirin
- One part water

1. Place peeled eggs in an airtight container, such as a hard plastic container or a sealable bag.
2. Pour the above ingredients into the container to cover (you may not need to use all the brine; reduce the amount as necessary).
3. Soak, jostling contents every hour or so, for 4-6 hours.
4. Remove from brine and reserve in the fridge until needed.
