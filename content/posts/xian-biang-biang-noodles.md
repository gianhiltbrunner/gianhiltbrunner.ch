Biang biang noodles are the belt-wide, hand-pulled noodles of Xi'an, seasoned with a pungent dressing of chilli, garlic and Sichuan pepper set alight by hot oil (a technique called *you po*, "oil pouring"). The dough is nothing more than flour, water and salt — the whole thing rests on patience and gluten development rather than any special ingredient. This is my adaptation of Wei Guo's recipe from Red House Spice, with one change: instead of flattening each piece by hand with a rolling pin, I sheet the rested dough on my Ono pasta machine. It gives me an even rectangle every time and takes the guesswork out of the step just before pulling.

## Sources

> Xi'an Biang Biang Noodles; Wei Guo, [Red House Spice](https://redhousespice.com/biang-biang-noodles/)

**Note on Equipment:**

For flattening I use the [Ono Type 1 Pasta Machine](https://www.shichifuku-tool.jp/item/imonoseimenki1gata/) — the same heavy-duty machine I use for low-hydration ramen noodles. This dough is a medium-firm 50% hydration, so it sheets cleanly without fighting the rollers. If you don't have a machine, a rolling pin works fine; the article this is adapted from is written around exactly that.

## The Dough

Makes 2 servings (8 noodle pieces)

- 250 grams all-purpose flour
- 125 grams water (50% hydration)
- 2 grams (¼ teaspoon) salt
- Cooking oil, for coating

A flour-water ratio of 2:1 by weight is the target. I use a plain all-purpose flour with a protein content around 10-11%. If all you have is bread flour (over 12%), cut it with a little cake flour — otherwise the dough turns too elastic and springs back to shape faster than you can pull it. The finished dough should be medium-firm and smooth; it softens as it rests, so don't chase softness at the mixing stage.

1. Add the salt to the water and stir to dissolve.
2. Put the flour in a mixing bowl. Pour the salted water over gradually while stirring with chopsticks, until the flour comes together into a rough, shaggy dough.
3. Cover and rest for 10-15 minutes. This first rest lets the flour hydrate fully and makes the next kneading almost effortless. Don't skip it.
4. Knead until you have a smooth, medium-firm dough. In a stand mixer with a dough hook, this is about 8 minutes on low speed.

**Why resting works:** the moment water meets flour, the individual gluten proteins begin to unravel and hook onto one another. Given time, they build an interconnected network of gluten strands — this is what holds the dough together and gives it the elasticity to stretch to a belt without tearing. Every rest in this recipe is buying you that network. Rush it and the dough fights back.

## Shape and Rest

- The dough from above
- Cooking oil, for coating

1. Divide the dough into 8 equal pieces. Knead each one briefly and roll into a small gherkin shape.
2. Coat each piece thoroughly in oil and lay them on a plate or in a container. The oil keeps a skin from forming and makes the pieces easier to sheet and pull later.
3. Cover with cling film and rest for at least 1 hour at room temperature.

> You can hold the oiled pieces in the fridge overnight for next-day noodles. Bring them fully back to room temperature before sheeting and pulling, or they'll resist the stretch.

## Sheet and Pull

- The rested dough pieces
- Potato or corn starch, for dusting

This is where the Ono comes in. Sheeting on the machine replaces the rolling-pin-flattening step in the traditional method; everything after that is the same hands-only pull.

1. Put a large pot of water on to boil. You want it going before you start pulling, because the noodles go from counter to pot immediately.
2. Working one piece at a time, run it through the Ono on the widest setting, then step down a notch or two until you have an even rectangle a few millimetres thick. Dust lightly with starch if it sticks. Don't sheet it too thin — you still need body to pull against.
3. Let the sheeted piece rest a moment. By the time you've sheeted the next one or two, the first will have relaxed and be ready to pull.
4. Press a chopstick or the edge of a rolling pin lengthwise down the middle of the rectangle to score a deep indentation. Be careful not to cut all the way through.
5. Hold each end and gently pull, working the piece longer and wider until it's your preferred thickness — anywhere up to belt-wide.
6. Lay the pulled noodle on the counter and tear it apart along the scored line, leaving the two halves joined at the far end to make one long loop.

## Cook

- The pulled noodles
- 1 head bok choy, or other leafy greens, separated into leaves

1. Drop each noodle into the boiling water the instant it's pulled — don't let it sit on the counter. Pull and cook the next while the first cooks.
2. Cook roughly 4 noodle pieces (one portion) at a time so the pot stays at a rolling boil. The noodles need only a couple of minutes; they're done when they float and turn glossy.
3. When the noodles are nearly there, add the bok choy and blanch for about 20 seconds.
4. Lift the noodles and greens straight into serving bowls, draining well. Repeat for the second portion.

## The Dressing (You Po)

Per serving:

- 1 tablespoon scallions, finely chopped
- 2 teaspoons garlic, minced
- Chilli flakes, to taste
- Chilli powder, to taste
- ¼ teaspoon ground Sichuan pepper
- Salt, to taste
- 3 tablespoons cooking oil
- 1 tablespoon light soy sauce
- 1 tablespoon black rice vinegar (Chinkiang)

The name *you po che mian* — "oil-poured hand-pulled noodles" — is the whole method in four words. The raw aromatics sit on top of the hot noodles and the smoking oil cooks them on contact, blooming the chilli and Sichuan pepper into fragrance all at once.

1. Pile the chopped scallions, minced garlic, chilli flakes, chilli powder, ground Sichuan pepper and a pinch of salt on top of the drained noodles.
2. Heat the oil in a small pan until it just starts to smoke.
3. Pour the smoking oil directly over the aromatics — it should sizzle and release its fragrance immediately.
4. Add the soy sauce and black rice vinegar, then toss everything together to coat the noodles evenly. Serve at once.
